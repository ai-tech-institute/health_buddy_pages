# Type 2 Diabetes: Managing Blood Sugar Emergencies

For primary caretakers, recognizing and quickly responding to blood sugar emergencies—hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) and hyperglycemia (high blood sugar)—is vital. Proactive steps can prevent serious complications.

## Hypoglycemia (Low Blood Sugar)

Occurs when blood glucose drops below 70 mg/dL.
**Signs:** Shakiness, sweating, rapid heartbeat, anxiety, hunger, dizziness. Untreated: confusion, blurred vision, slurred speech, seizures, unconsciousness. Note: some medications (e.g., beta-blockers) can mask symptoms; monitor closely.
**Response (Rule of 15):**
1.  Consume 15g fast-acting carbs (e.g., 4oz juice, 5-6 hard candies).
2.  Wait 15 minutes, recheck blood sugar.
3.  Repeat if still below 70 mg/dL.
Once normalized, have a small snack if the next meal is over an hour away. For severe hypoglycemia (unconscious or unable to swallow), administer prescribed glucagon. Training to administer glucagon is crucial; discuss this with your doctor, a diabetes educator, or pharmacist. Newer FDA-approved options like Baqsimi (nasal powder) and Gvoke (pre-mixed auto-injector/syringe) are for severe hypoglycemia. Discuss these with your healthcare provider for prescription, proper instruction, and potential side effects (e.g., nausea, vomiting, headache). **Always call 911 immediately after administering glucagon.**

## Hyperglycemia (High Blood Sugar)

Occurs when blood glucose is above 180 mg/dL. Prolonged high blood sugar can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a medical emergency.
**Signs:** Increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, headache, blurred vision. Worsening (DKA): nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fruity-smelling breath, deep, rapid breathing.
**Response:**
1.  Check blood sugar frequently.
2.  Drink plenty of sugar-free fluids to prevent dehydration.
3.  If consistently above 250 mg/dL, check for ketones using urine strips or a blood ketone meter. Moderate to large ketones signal DKA risk.
4.  Administer a **correction dose of insulin *only* if prescribed as part of a specific, pre-established plan developed with the healthcare provider. Never make this decision independently.**

## When to Seek Immediate Help: Doctor vs. 911

*   **Call the Doctor:**
    *   **Hypoglycemia:** Frequent mild-to-moderate lows, or blood sugar unresponsive to repeated "Rule of 15."
    *   **Hyperglycemia:** Persistent highs (e.g., >250 mg/dL for several readings) without DKA symptoms, or if the sick day plan isn't effective.
*   **Call 911:**
    *   **Hypoglycemia:** Unconscious, unable to swallow, seizure, or after administering glucagon.
    *   **Hyperglycemia:** Moderate to large ketones *with* DKA symptoms (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fruity breath, confusion), or severe dehydration.

## Practical Guidance & Prevention

*   **Medication Awareness:** Understand the patient's specific diabetes medications, including dosages, timing, proper storage, common side effects (some may mimic blood sugar symptoms), and potential drug interactions. Discuss these thoroughly with the healthcare team.
*   **Glucose Monitoring:** Learn how to use a glucometer or interpret CGM data. Discuss target blood sugar ranges with the healthcare provider.
*   **Sick Day Plan:** Work with the healthcare team to develop clear guidelines for managing blood sugar during illness, including medication adjustments.
*   **Emergency Contacts:** Keep a list of emergency contacts, including the patient's doctor, readily accessible.
*   **Proactive Prevention:** To minimize emergencies, ensure consistent meal times, strict medication adherence, and regular blood sugar monitoring. Always have fast-acting carbohydrates readily available.
